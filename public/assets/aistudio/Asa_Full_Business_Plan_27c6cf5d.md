# Asa (آسا) — Full Business Plan

**As of:** 12 October 2026  
**Prepared for:** Founder decision-making  
**Status:** Evidence-based planning document; not legal, tax, investment, or medical advice.

## 1. Strategic conclusion

Asa should preserve a broad brand vision—family support, companionship, and peace of mind—but launch narrowly as a **trusted Tehran-based family-support operator**. The first commercial wedge should combine two separately bookable services: custom shopping and non-clinical medical appointment accompaniment. The brand promise is broader than the initial catalog; the operating promise is deliberately narrower.

The recommendation is **proceed conditionally**. Commercial feasibility is plausible. Payment feasibility is not currently verified and must be treated as a launch blocker.

## 2. Payment-feasibility gate

The reviewed official sources support three conclusions:

- Germany, Canada, the UK, Australia, and Turkey have no demonstrated blanket ban on ordinary grocery shopping and genuinely non-clinical accompaniment performed in Iran. This is conditional, not clearance.
- The United States should not be marketed for this model now. U.S.-person and U.S.-nexus Iran rules broadly restrict unauthorized Iran-related services, and the personal-remittance authority is noncommercial rather than a clear authorization for Asa’s commercial fee.
- No reviewed provider is presently evidenced as an approved end-to-end route for the disclosed flow. Stripe, PayPal, Wise, Coinbase Commerce/Business, and BitPay publish restrictions that conflict with it. Adyen requires case-specific review and does not publish affirmative Iran-flow eligibility.

The payment architecture is therefore **redesign/no-go today**. Asa must not conceal the Iran connection, use a third-party account, misdescribe the service, split transactions, or treat crypto as a workaround. A later conditional launch may be possible in selected non-U.S. markets only after written provider and settlement-bank approval for the exact entity, parties, service scope, and funds flow.

## 3. Market and customer evidence

The commercial problem is real but not yet quantified with a defensible bottom-up market size. Public evidence shows:

- Iranian diaspora communities are concentrated in the U.S., Canada, Germany, the UK, and Turkey; the Migration Policy Institute cites 2019 UN estimates of approximately 385,000 Iranian immigrants in the U.S., 164,000 in Canada, 127,000 in Germany, 90,000 in the UK, and 83,000 in Turkey.[1]
- Iranian caregiver studies describe physical, mental, social, financial, and system-level burdens, including the time and cost of doctor visits and inadequate remote support.[2]
- A 2024 south-Tehran study specifically recommends affordable eldercare and home-care services aligned with caregiver training needs.[3]
- A systematic review of age-friendly indicators found that Iranian cities generally did not meet required standards, with poor performance in respect, social acceptance, civic participation, and employment.[4]

These sources indicate **need and service gaps**, not willingness to pay. Asa must validate payment behavior through a paid pilot, not likes, interviews alone, or diaspora population totals.

### Priority segments

| Segment | Problem | Current substitutes | Purchase trigger | Asa implication |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Diaspora adult children | Cannot manage a parent’s urgent task in person | Relatives, driver, local shop, informal helper | Appointment, illness, travel, missed routine | Highest priority; trust/reporting are central |
| Adult children in another Iranian city | Distance within Iran creates practical gaps | Family network, taxi, shop delivery | Repeated errands or medical visit | Test after Tehran process is reliable |
| Families with an independent older adult | Need occasional help without institutional care | Relatives, caregiver, local providers | Mobility decline or caregiver fatigue | Strong later segment; safeguarding required |
| Busy local households | Lack time for shopping and logistics | Apps, local shops, drivers | Time pressure | Lower differentiation; do not lead with it |
| Gift purchasers | Want a useful act of care | Food/gift delivery sites | Birthday, Nowruz, crisis, surprise | Acquisition channel and add-on, not core wedge |

### Customer roles

The **payer** is often abroad. The **recipient** is in Iran. The **requester** may be a sibling or relative. The **approver** may be another family member. Asa must design evidence and consent for all four roles.

## 4. Competitive landscape

The meaningful alternatives are not only “care companies.” They include:

- **Iranian gift/food delivery sites:** SendGiftToIran accepts foreign orders and arranges local restaurant delivery in Iran, positioning food as a practical emotional connection.[5]
- **Broad diaspora gifting platforms:** ShopiPersia markets locally sourced gifts, groceries, household essentials, and health/wellness items with international card, PayPal, and crypto claims.[6] Those claims are competitor marketing, not proof that Asa can use the same routes; Asa should not copy them without provider verification.
- **Local online grocery platforms:** Tehran alternatives include Snapp Market, Okala, DigiKala Jet, and local shops. DigiKala Jet is described as a fast grocery marketplace in Tehran/Karaj; local shops may take phone orders and offer flexible payment.[7]
- **Informal networks:** relatives, drivers, neighbors, building managers, clinic staff, and local shopkeepers. These are often cheaper and trusted, but inconsistent and difficult to audit.
- **Care and home-health providers:** may offer more clinical capability than Asa but can be expensive, poorly suited to a single cross-border task, or not designed for foreign-family reporting.

Asa should not claim an uncontested market. Its realistic differentiation is **accountable coordination plus evidence of completion**, not a new logistics technology.

## 5. Launch service catalog and naming architecture

The customer-facing service family should use the founder’s Persian naming architecture:

- **آسا همراه / Asa Hamrah:** accompanying family members for practical tasks and travel/transport needs.
- **آسا خوراک / Asa Khoraak:** packaged food, varied meals, and daily or bulk meal plans delivered through authorized local suppliers.
- **آسا پزشک / Asa Pezeshk:** medical-family coordination such as booking appointments, follow-up, and practical accompaniment to treatment centers; non-clinical unless separately licensed and approved.

The launch remains narrower than the full brand architecture: initially offer the reliably deliverable parts of آسا همراه and آسا پزشک in one Tehran zone, and validate آسا خوراک through an authorized food-supplier pilot before promising recurring or bulk meal delivery.

### Available at launch — only after gates pass

**آسا همراه — Shopping and practical accompaniment / Asa Hamrah**

Includes: list review, budget and substitution rules, local purchase, item check, delivery, receipt/photo evidence where lawful and consented, and a factual completion update.

Excludes: unlimited product search, controlled medicines, cash handling outside approved procedures, clinical advice, guaranteed availability, and unapproved out-of-zone deliveries.

Suggested pricing logic: service fee + delivery/travel fee; grocery cost is pass-through. Use a minimum service fee to protect small orders.

**آسا پزشک — Medical-family coordination and non-clinical accompaniment / Asa Pezeshk**

Includes: appointment coordination, practical visit plan, arrival/registration/waiting-room support, suitable transport coordination, accompaniment for a defined time window, and an authorized factual update.

Excludes: diagnosis, treatment, medication administration, clinical decisions, interpretation of results, medical representation, lifting/transfers unless specifically trained and insured, and emergency care.

Suggested pricing logic: fixed package for a defined service window, additional-hour charge, transport pass-through, and cancellation rules.

**آسا خوراک — Packaged meals and food plans / Asa Khoraak**

Initial scope: packaged meals, varied food selections, daily meal plans, and bulk packages sourced and delivered through authorized local suppliers. The service must define food safety, supplier responsibility, allergens, delivery windows, substitutions, refunds, recipient preferences, and whether the service is a marketplace, agent, or principal seller.

### Coming soon

- Asa Hamrah expansion: scheduled non-medical visits, walks, errands, and social connection.
- Recurring shopping bundles.
- Asa Khoraak recurring and bulk meal packages after supplier and food-safety validation.

### Long-term opportunities

- Multi-city coverage.
- Corporate/family-benefit partnerships.
- More formal care coordination, only with licensing, insurance, training, and privacy controls.
- Technology-assisted family dashboard after repeatable operations exist.

## 6. Operating model

Use an asset-light model. Do not warehouse groceries or build a custom app. The minimum operating stack is a bilingual website/form, order spreadsheet or lightweight CRM, business phone/WhatsApp only where privacy and consent are appropriate, receipt storage, staff scheduling, incident log, and payment reconciliation.

### Standard Shopping workflow

1. Customer submits recipient location, list, budget, substitutions, timing, and consent.
2. Coordinator confirms scope and price.
3. Approved worker shops from a defined supplier set.
4. Worker checks items, records substitutions, and obtains receipt.
5. Delivery is confirmed with recipient.
6. Customer receives an itemized reconciliation and factual update.

### Standard Medical Companion workflow

1. Confirm appointment, recipient consent, emergency contact, mobility needs, language, and exclusions.
2. Confirm transport and arrival plan.
3. Worker meets recipient at agreed location with identification.
4. Worker supports logistics only and documents observable facts.
5. Worker escalates any emergency to emergency services and the designated contact.
6. Customer receives a factual update without medical interpretation.

### Trust and safety controls

- Identity verification and references for all workers.
- Role-specific training in boundaries, safeguarding, consent, incident escalation, dignity, and privacy.
- No unsupervised access to sensitive information beyond what is necessary.
- Two-person approval for refunds or unusual cash/beneficiary requests.
- Visit check-in/check-out and worker location confirmation within a defined policy.
- Complaint, refund, substitution, missed-visit, and incident procedures.
- Insurance and local legal review before paid work.
- Optional photos only with explicit recipient/customer consent.

## 8. Pricing and unit economics

Do not use cost-plus pricing alone. Price against the value metric: **a completed, accountable service window with evidence**, not the grocery basket value.

Initial test prices in the model are planning assumptions only. Run three price cells per service during the pilot. Measure conversion, completion, contribution margin, complaints, and repeat purchase. Avoid automatic subscriptions until repeat behavior is observed.

## 9. Scenario economics

The accompanying workbook models conservative, base, and upside cases. The model separates gross billings, product pass-through, service revenue, direct variable costs, fixed operating costs, contribution profit, and cash flow.

Key sensitivities are: payment fees and failure rates, worker compensation, travel time, order volume, repeat purchase, medical visit utilization, refunds, and customer acquisition cost. The business should stop or redesign if paid orders do not show positive contribution after direct service costs, or if payment/compliance costs exceed the service margin.

## 10. Marketing strategy in brief

Lead with a specific problem: “I need someone reliable in Tehran to help my parent with this task, and I need to know it was completed.” Do not lead with a vague promise to “take care of everything.” Avoid guilt, fear, helplessness, and unsupported safety claims.

Priority channels:

1. Referral and warm diaspora networks.
2. Search intent around sending groceries/help to parents in Iran.
3. Persian-language community groups and creators, subject to platform and payment compliance.
4. Authorized local partnerships with clinics, laboratories, and suppliers.
5. Retention and family reorders.

## 11. Website and technology

Launch with a fast bilingual site, two service pages, service-area checker, transparent price logic, request form, consent notice, FAQ, support contact, and order-status communication. Avoid custom apps, complex portals, and automated payment promises before the payment route is cleared.

## 12. Risks and decision gates

| Risk | Severity | Likelihood | Response |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Payment provider refusal or account closure | 5 | 4 | Treat as launch blocker; get written approval before orders |
| Sanctions/AML breach | 5 | 3 | Counsel, screening, documented funds flow, stop controls |
| Worker misconduct or safeguarding incident | 5 | 3 | Vetting, training, insurance, two-way check-ins, escalation |
| Service scope drifts into regulated healthcare | 4 | 3 | Explicit exclusions, staff scripts, legal review |
| Poor unit economics | 4 | 3 | Minimum fees, zone limits, paid pilot, stop thresholds |
| Low repeat demand | 3 | 3 | Test specific use cases and bundles before subscription |
| FX/inflation exposure | 4 | 4 | Quote service fee in customer currency, reprice frequently, avoid holding inventory |

## 13. Final answers to the strategic questions

1. **Strongest proposition:** accountable local family support with receipts, accompaniment, and an authorized update.
2. **Lead service:** test Shopping and Medical Companion together in the brand, but make them separate offers and measure separately.
3. **Best segment:** diaspora adult children with a parent in Tehran and a time-sensitive task.
4. **Launch area:** a narrow Tehran zone, conditional on worker and supplier interviews.
5. **Priority markets:** non-U.S. diaspora markets only after payment clearance; Germany, Canada, UK, Australia, and Turkey are candidates, not approved markets.
6. **International payment model:** not viable on current evidence.
7. **Simplest architecture:** no launch until a named provider/bank approves the disclosed principal-service flow; do not improvise a crypto/remittance route.
8. **Purchase model:** individual purchases first; prepaid bundles later; subscriptions only after observed repeat demand.
9. **Pricing:** fixed service fee plus transparent pass-through and additional-hour rules.
10. **Differentiation:** accountability, non-clinical scope, family reporting, and repeatable quality controls.
11. **Repeat quality:** on-time completion, factual reports, itemized receipts, low complaint/refund rates, and reliable support.
12. **Before first order:** payment, legal, insurance, staff, consent, privacy, price, and incident controls.
13. **Startup budget:** plan on $10,000–$20,000 before specialist compliance/legal costs; validate locally.
14. **Expansion evidence:** 20–30 paid orders, positive contribution margin, repeat demand, reliable worker utilization, and zero severe incidents.
15. **Most uncertain assumptions:** payment approval, Iranian local permissions, actual willingness to pay, worker supply, repeat frequency, and service-level margins.
